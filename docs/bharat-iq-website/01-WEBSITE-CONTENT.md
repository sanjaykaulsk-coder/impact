**H2:** What Is A CAPI Survey, And Why Does Rural India Depend On It?

CAPI — Computer-Assisted Personal Interviewing — is a face-to-face survey conducted by a trained interviewer on a tablet or phone. In rural India it is the primary method because many respondents are not comfortable reading and answering a questionnaire on their own, connectivity is inconsistent, and dialect-fluent human interviewing produces far better data quality than remote methods. CAPI also allows offline data capture, GPS stamping, photo evidence and real-time validation checks — which is how rural fieldwork gets audited rather than trusted.

---

**H2:** How A Bharat IQ Study Runs
1. **Business question first (Week 0)** — we refuse to scope a study until we know which decision it will change.
2. **Design & sampling (Week 1)** — instrument design, dialect adaptation, sampling frame drawn from our village and retailer database.
3. **Pilot (Week 2)** — 20–30 interviews, instrument correction, interviewer recalibration. Never skipped.
4. **Fieldwork** — trained local teams, geo-tagged digital capture, daily quality dashboards, supervisory back-checks.
5. **Analysis** — weighting, cross-tabs, segmentation, significance testing. Every number carries its base size.

**H2:** Six Things About The Bharat Consumer That Most Brands Get Wrong

**1. Rural is not one market.** A market is defined by irrigation, crop cycle, migration pattern, road access and proximity to a mandi town — not by a state boundary. Two districts in the same state routinely behave like different countries.

**2. "Price-conscious" is not the same as "cheap-seeking."** Rural buyers are value-calculating, not value-abandoning. The rural middle class — a majority of rural consumption — increasingly buys on quality and brand `[VERIFY]`. Getting this wrong produces the classic error: a stripped-down "rural variant" that the market reads as an insult.

**3. Trust is chained, not broadcast.** The shopkeeper's recommendation, a neighbour's experience and a visible demonstration outrank a television commercial. Experiential contact — touch, feel, demo — does more work in rural information search than advertising does.

**4. The buying calendar is agricultural, not corporate.** Harvest, procurement payment, festival and wedding seasons drive purchase capacity. A national Q3 push that lands before the money arrives is a wasted quarter.

**5. The decision is joint.** Women decide far more rural categories than the standard "male head of household" interview captures. Research the wrong respondent and the finding is confidently wrong.

**6. Baskets are widening, not just growing.** Average rural basket sizes have expanded sharply in recent years `[VERIFY: 5.8 items in 2022 → 9.3 in 2024]` — a category-mix change, not a volume change, and it demands a different portfolio response.

---

**H2:** How We Get To Real Insight

**Observe before you ask.** Pantry audits, shelf checks and accompanied purchases before stated-preference questions.
**Go where the buying happens.** Haats, mandis, weekly markets, melas and the kirana counter — not a central location test in a district HQ.
**Interview the influencer, not only the buyer.** The retailer, the mechanic, the agri-input dealer, the ASHA worker, the SHG leader, the village elder — whoever the category's trust actually runs through.
**Separate demand from distribution.** A product nobody buys and a product nobody can find look identical in sales data. They need opposite solutions. We test which one you have before recommending anything.

**FAQ**

**Q: How is rural consumer behaviour different from urban in India?**
Rural purchase decisions rely more on personal trust, retailer recommendation and physical demonstration than on advertising; buying capacity follows agricultural and festival cycles rather than salary cycles; decisions are more often joint household decisions; regional-language communication is materially more effective; and consumers evaluate a product's total value — durability, refill cost, resale — rather than only its sticker price.

**Q: Is "rural" the same as "Bharat"?**
Not quite. "Rural" is a census definition based on population, density and occupation. "Bharat" is a market definition — rural plus small-town and Tier 2–4 India, which share consumption patterns, trust structures and media habits that differ from metro India. Most brands are better served by planning for Bharat than by the rural/urban census line.

**Q: Can online panels be used for rural research in India?**
Only for the connected, semi-urban edge — and even there with caution. Online panels systematically over-represent younger, male, smartphone-fluent, road-connected respondents and under-represent interior rural India. For most rural decisions they produce a confident, unrepresentative answer. Mixed-mode designs that use CAPI for rural and online for connected Tier 2 are the defensible approach.

**H2:** Addressable Is Not Reachable
A district with 900 outlets and no viable distributor economics is not a market yet. A district with 400 outlets on a serviceable route is. Every Bharat IQ sizing output separates:
- **Addressable** — total category demand that exists
- **Reachable** — what your current or planned structure can physically service
- **Winnable** — what you can take from competition at a defensible cost to serve

Plans built on the first number fail. Plans built on the third one work.

**FAQ**

**Q: How do you size a rural market in India?**
Bottom-up: start from the village and outlet universe in the target geography, apply category penetration and consumption rates from primary research or benchmark data, adjust for seasonality and income cycles, then separate addressable demand from what your distribution can actually reach. Top-down methods — national category value split by rural share and state population — are fast but routinely wrong at district level, which is where the actual decision gets made.

**Q: What village-level data is available in India?**
Census 2011 remains the base administrative dataset (village directory, amenities, demographics), supplemented by agricultural statistics, MoSPI surveys, electrification and connectivity data, and banking and telecom penetration data. It is dated and incomplete for commercial use, which is why Bharat IQ maintains its own continuously updated village and retailer database built from live field operations.
