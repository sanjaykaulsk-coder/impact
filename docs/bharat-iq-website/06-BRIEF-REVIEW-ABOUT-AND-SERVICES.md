Substantially better than the earlier draft, and better than the Stitch output by a wide margin. ## 1. THE FACTUAL PROBLEM — the About page breaks its own rule in its opening paragraph

> **The Problem It Solves:** *"Rural India is not one market. It is 640 districts, 6,500 blocks, 6 lakh villages, and 38,000 weekly haats…"*

> **What Makes It Different:** *"Every number carries its source, its vintage and its confidence."*

The second sentence is the brand's core promise. The first sentence breaks it — four unlabelled numbers, three of which are Census 2011 vintage, describing rural India as it is *today*.

**640 districts is the Census 2011 count.** India has roughly **784–802 districts in 2026** (sources vary as states create new ones). The figure is fifteen years stale.

It also **contradicts your own Services page**, which says `765 districts with verified code-level geography`. 765 and 640 are not the same number, and neither is the current national count. Three different district figures are now in circulation across two documents.

**6 lakh villages** is Census 2011 inhabited villages (~5.94–5.98 lakh, depending on the table). Correct as a rounding, but same vintage issue.

**6,500 blocks** matches neither cleanly — Census 2011 recorded 5,924 sub-districts (5,767 tehsils); current block counts are often cited nearer 7,000. Confirm the source before publishing.

**Why this matters more than the arithmetic:** the opening paragraph is where a sceptical reader decides whether to believe the rest. If your first four numbers are unsourced and stale on a page whose central claim is *every number carries its vintage*, the claim reads as marketing rather than method.

**Fix — one line:**
> *"Rural India is not one market. It is roughly 800 districts, 6 lakh villages and around 47,000 weekly haats (Census 2011 and LGD, 2026), each with its own economics, access and rhythm."*

Sourcing your own problem statement is the cheapest possible demonstration that the honesty claim is real.

## 2. THE 38,000 HAATS PROBLEM — one number doing two different jobs

It appears twice on the About page, meaning two different things:

- **Problem statement:** *"…and 38,000 weekly haats"* → reads as **India's national total**
- **Locate:** *"Maps the real ground: 38,000 haats…"* → is **Bharat IQ's mapped count**

India has approximately **47,000** rural weekly haats. So the problem statement both understates India and, more importantly, **gives away your single best proof point** — because if 38,000 is the national total, mapping 38,000 sounds routine rather than remarkable.

**Fix, and this is an upgrade not just a correction:**
> Problem statement: *"…and around 47,000 weekly haats."*
> Locate: *"We have mapped 38,000 of India's ~47,000 weekly haats — with the market day for each."*

That is 82% of the national universe with a field-collected attribute nobody else holds. Stated that way it becomes the strongest sentence on the page. Stated the current way, it disappears.

*(Confirm the market-day attribute is populated for all 38,000, not a subset. If it covers 31,000, say 31,000 — still remarkable.)*

### 3.1 On "real invoices"

P1 said the cost data is built on *"real invoices and estimates"*. P2 says *"a realistic range drawn from real invoices"* and *"invoices that were paid"*.

If the corpus is a mix, **"invoices and vendor estimates" is the accurate phrase and it costs you nothing** — it is still vastly stronger than a rate card. "Invoices that were paid" is a beautiful line, but only if every record is one. This is exactly the small drift that a client discovers in a demo, and the whole positioning is built on not doing that.

## 4. THE NAMING CALL — verbs or nouns

P1 names four products. P2 names four verbs. Both are good; they are different taxonomies and you cannot ship both as navigation.

**Recommendation: verbs on the website, product names inside the product.**

*Prioritise · Plan · Locate · Cost* tells a visitor what they get in four words with no explanation needed. *Priority Engine · Campaign Intelligence · Cost Intelligence · Bharat Data* is what the modules are called once someone is inside and needs to know which screen they are on.

**But fix the order.** Both pages should run in the same sequence, and it should be the buyer's sequence: **Locate → Prioritise → Plan → Cost** *(the ground, then where, then how, then what it costs)* — or keep Prioritise first if you want to lead with the decision rather than the map. Either is defensible. Two different orders across two pages is not.

One note: *"Locate"* is the weakest of the four verbs — it undersells the largest asset. **"Map"** or **"Find the ground"** carries more. Consider it.

## 5. "AN ENGINE THAT REASONS" — the one claim without evidence attached

> *"Not a research report. Not a dashboard. An engine that reasons."*
> Tone note: *"no jargon about AI, no promises of certainty."*

Category definition by negation is strong, and "not a dashboard" is a useful shot at the incumbents. But **"reasons" is an AI claim in plain clothes**, and it sits directly above an instruction to avoid AI jargon.

It is also the only sentence in either document that asserts a capability with nothing attached. Everything else — 38,000 haats, invoices across 25 states, 27 years — carries its evidence. "Reasons" does not.

**Decide it deliberately, three options:**
- **If there is a model in the loop:** say so plainly. *"It reads your brief, breaks it into an execution plan, and prices it against real invoices."* Describing the mechanism is more convincing than the word "reasons" and more honest than avoiding the subject.
- **If it is rules, benchmarks and scoring:** *"An engine that works it out"* or *"It does the reasoning a good planner would, against evidence a planner doesn't have."*
- **If unsure:** cut the sentence. *"Not a research report. Not a dashboard."* stands perfectly well alone.

## 6. THE LEAD LINE — promote the supporting line

> Proposed lead: *"Intelligence on Bharat. Marketing, reach and the cost of getting there."*
> Proposed support: *"Where to go. How to reach. What it costs."*

**These are the wrong way round.** The lead is abstract — "Intelligence on Bharat" says nothing a competitor couldn't say, and "Marketing, reach and the cost of getting there" is a list rather than a promise.

**"Where to go. How to reach. What it costs."** is the better line by a distance: three beats, plain words, and it *is* the product — it maps directly onto Prioritise, Plan and Cost. It also answers the buyer's actual question in the buyer's actual words.

**Recommended hero:**
> # Where to go. How to reach. What it costs.
> Intelligence on Bharat, built on 27 years of what actually happened on the ground.

That keeps both lines and puts the concrete one first.

## 7. AUDIENCE VS CAPABILITY — apply your own rule

"Who it is for" includes *"Government and development organisations running behaviour-change programmes."*

But all four modules are described in commercial terms throughout both pages — detergent, confectionery, biscuits, lubricants, retailers, distributors, category. Nothing in Prioritise / Plan / Locate / Cost speaks to IEC, SBCC, beneficiary reach or behaviour change. *(P1 does mention development-sector programmes as a data source inside Campaign Intelligence — that is a source, not a capability.)*

Your own closing rule — *don't publish capability that isn't live yet* — applies to **audiences as well as modules**. A development-sector visitor who arrives and finds nothing addressed to them bounces, and you have spent the credibility for nothing.

**Either** add one line under each module showing the development-sector equivalent (*"…or which blocks to prioritise for a sanitation programme"*), **or** hold that audience back until there is something to show them. Both are fine. Claiming them with nothing behind it is not.

## 8. WHAT IS GENUINELY EXCELLENT — keep these exactly as written

1. **"The data comes from vans that ran, haats that were covered, retailers that were onboarded, and invoices that were paid."**
   The best sentence across both documents. Concrete, specific, and structurally impossible for a competitor to say. This belongs on the homepage, not buried in a differentiator list.

2. **"In rural marketing, a confident wrong number costs more than an honest gap."**
   The whole positioning in one line.

3. **"It says what it doesn't know"** — as the **first** differentiator.
   Brave and correct. In a category where everyone overclaims, visible restraint is the differentiator.

4. **"Prosperity is not the same as reachability. A district where households can afford your product but you cannot service it is not a priority market."**
   A real methodological position, and a direct attack on every competitor selling one blended rural score.

5. **"A plan for biscuits is not a plan for lubricants."** and **"The same district ranks differently for detergent than for confectionery, because the category decides."**
   Two versions of the same idea; both good. Pick one per page.

6. **"An honest estimate, not a guess dressed as a quotation."**
   Keep *estimate* everywhere in copy and UI. Never *quote*.

## 9. WHAT'S MISSING FROM BOTH PAGES

**a) What the reader does next.** Neither page has a CTA. For an *engine*, "Request a Briefing" — the CTA in the earlier pack — is now wrong; it suits a consultancy. The natural ask is:

> **"See it run on your category."**

That is a demo request, it is specific, and it is confident in exactly the way the product claims to be. It also sets up your under-promise/over-deliver rule: the site says less than the demo shows.

**b) Proof of the honesty claim.** *"It says what it doesn't know"* is asserted but never shown. **One screenshot of a real output** — a district scored with a low-confidence flag and a stated evidence gap visible on screen — would do more than the entire differentiator section. It is also the one thing a competitor cannot screenshot back at you, because their product does not do it.

Show it. Don't describe it.

## CLOSING

The instinct behind this revision is right, and it is the instinct that matters most for this business: fewer numbers, rounded down, honesty promoted from footnote to headline, and a stated preference for the site under-promising what the demo delivers.

The one thing to hold onto: **your opening paragraph has to obey your own rule.** A page that promises every number carries its source, vintage and confidence — and then opens with four unsourced Census-2011 figures — hands a sceptical reader the exact objection you have spent the rest of the page disarming. It is a one-line fix, and it is the difference between a claim and a demonstration.
